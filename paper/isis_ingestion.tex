\section{What this document is}

This is the briefing for anyone who has to understand, run, or debug the HATI
ingestion pipeline without a background in planetary data processing. It covers why
the pipeline exists, what each piece of software in it does, what every line it
prints on the console means, and what the code is doing while it prints them. No
prior knowledge is assumed, and every technical term is defined the first time it
appears and again in the glossary at the back.

The short version: NASA archives raw images from a camera in orbit around the Moon.
Those files are not usable as pictures. This pipeline turns a chosen set of them into
calibrated, map-projected images of the same patch of ground, all aligned with each
other to better than one pixel, which is the input the hazard measurement needs.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- why
\section{Why the pipeline exists}

HATI is a hazard map for lunar landing sites. It has to find rocks that are smaller
than a pixel, at a place where the Sun never climbs more than a few degrees above the
horizon.

The target here is a point near Nobile crater in the lunar south polar region, at
\SI{84.79}{\degree} south. A lander touching down there needs to know whether the
patch underneath it holds a \SI{30}{\centi\meter} boulder. The best imagery available
comes from the Narrow Angle Camera on Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, which resolves
about half a metre to a metre per pixel from orbit. A \SI{30}{\centi\meter} rock is
therefore invisible. It occupies less than one pixel and changes that pixel's
brightness by almost nothing.

So we do not look for the rock. We look for its shadow, which at this latitude is
enormous.

\subsection{The shadow trick}

A vertical object of height $h$ lit by a Sun at elevation angle $e$ casts a shadow of
length $L$:

\begin{equation}
L \;=\; h \cdot \cot(e)
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
h \;=\; L \cdot \tan(e)
\end{equation}

Near the equator this is useless. The Sun climbs high, $\cot(e)$ falls toward zero,
and a small rock casts almost no shadow. At \SI{84.8}{\degree} south the Sun tops out
a few degrees above the horizon and the geometry inverts in our favour. At a Sun
elevation of \SI{5}{\degree}, $\cot(\SI{5}{\degree})$ is 11.4.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}S[table-format=1.2] S[table-format=2.0] S[table-format=2.2] S[table-format=2.1] l@{}}
\toprule
{Object height (m)} & {Sun elevation (\si{\degree})} & {Shadow length (m)} & {Shadow (px)} & Measurable? \\
\midrule
0.30 & 45 &  0.30 &  0.3 & No \\
0.30 & 10 &  1.70 &  1.9 & Marginal \\
0.30 &  5 &  3.43 &  3.8 & Yes \\
0.30 &  2 &  8.59 &  9.5 & Yes \\
1.00 &  5 & 11.43 & 12.7 & Yes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A rock a third of a metre across, invisible in its own right, throws a dark streak
four pixels long. That streak is measurable. The pipeline targets a Sun elevation
band of \SI{1.5}{\degree} to \SI{7.5}{\degree} for exactly this reason, aiming at
\SI{5}{\degree}, which is the compromise between long shadows and enough light to see
anything at all.

\begin{plainbox}
You cannot photograph the rock, so you photograph the stripe of darkness it throws
across the ground. Near the pole the Sun is so low that the stripe is more than ten
times longer than the rock is tall. The geometry does the magnification for you.
\end{plainbox}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- sweep
\section{Why one image is not enough}

Dark pixels are ambiguous. A shadow is dark. So is a patch of low-albedo soil, or a
small depression. From a single frame there is no way to tell a hazard from a stain,
and a hazard map built on stains would route a lander around obstacles that are not
there and into ones that are.

The discriminator is motion. Move the Sun and a real shadow swings around its caster
like a clock hand, staying anchored at the object's base. A dark patch of soil does
not move at all, because it is painted on the ground.

That is the \emph{solar sweep}. Collect frames of the same ground across a wide
spread of Sun azimuths, find every dark blob in every frame, and cast a vote at the
up-Sun end of each one, which is where the caster must be. Votes from a real obstacle
pile up on a single point. Votes from an albedo patch smear into a ring, because the
assumed caster position rotates with the Sun while the patch stays put.

Two consequences fall straight out of this, and the code enforces both. What matters
is the spread of Sun azimuth rather than the number of frames, so the pipeline
requires at least four frames covering at least \SI{40}{\degree} of azimuth and
refuses to continue otherwise. And the frames must be aligned to sub-pixel accuracy,
because the whole method rests on deciding whether a blob moved between two images.

\begin{keepbox}[Why alignment is the whole game]
If two frames are misaligned by more than a pixel, then a shadow that appears to move
by a pixel between them might be the shadow moving or might be the frame moving. The
method cannot separate the two, and the measurement is worthless. That is why this
pipeline ends in a hard pass-or-fail test rather than a picture.
\end{keepbox}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- isis
\section{What ISIS is, and why raw data is not a picture}

ISIS is the Integrated Software for Imagers and Spectrometers, written and maintained
by the USGS Astrogeology Science Center. It is the standard toolchain for turning
planetary spacecraft data into something scientifically usable, and almost every
published measurement from this camera passed through it.

What you download from the NASA Planetary Data System is an \emph{EDR}, an Experiment
Data Record. It is close to a raw dump of what the detector read out, and it has
three properties that make it useless as an image.

\begin{itemize}
\item The numbers are detector counts, not brightness. They carry the sensor's dark
current, its per-pixel gain variation, and an electronic echo from the readout. Two
frames of identical ground taken on different orbits will not have the same values.
\item There is no geometry attached. The file does not know where the spacecraft was,
where it was pointing, or where the Sun was. It is a rectangle of numbers with a
timestamp.
\item The camera is a \emph{line-scan} instrument. It has no shutter and takes no
snapshot. It builds an image one row at a time as the spacecraft flies, so every row
was taken from a slightly different place at a slightly different attitude. The raw
frame is not a projection of anything.
\end{itemize}

ISIS fixes all three, in that order. It runs on Linux and macOS only, which is why
this stage of HATI runs under WSL2 on a Windows machine.

One term worth having up front. A \emph{cube} is ISIS's own file format, ending in
\texttt{.cub}. It holds the image data together with a long text label carrying
everything ISIS knows about the frame. \emph{Level 1} means calibrated but still in
camera geometry. \emph{Level 2} means projected onto a map. The pipeline keeps only
level 2 and deletes the intermediates, because each frame is a quarter of a gigabyte.

\section{The ten stages}

Every stage prints a line beginning \texttt{\#\#STAGE} so the mission dashboard can
follow progress without parsing English. Four of the ten are ISIS programs. The rest
is HATI's own code.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}c l l p{6.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
& \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}Stage & \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}Owner
& \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}In one line \\
\midrule
1 & \texttt{SELECT}      & HATI & Choose which frames to fetch \\
2 & \texttt{DOWNLOAD}    & HATI & Fetch the raw file from the archive \\
3 & \texttt{LRONAC2ISIS} & ISIS & Translate the archive file into an ISIS cube \\
4 & \texttt{SPICEINIT}   & ISIS & Attach the spacecraft and Sun geometry \\
5 & \texttt{CAMPT}       & HATI & Ask the camera whether the site is on the detector \\
6 & \texttt{LRONACCAL}   & ISIS & Convert detector counts into real brightness \\
7 & \texttt{CAM2MAP}     & ISIS & Project the frame onto the map grid \\
8 & \texttt{COREGISTER}  & HATI & Measure how far the frame is from truth \\
9 & \texttt{GATE}        & HATI & Pass or fail the whole stack \\
10 & \texttt{MANIFEST}   & HATI & Hand the survivors to the shadow detector \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Stage 1, SELECT}
Reads the sweep catalogue and chooses which frames to fetch. It splits the compass
into bins and picks one frame per bin, so the set covers many Sun directions rather
than clustering at one. It rejects any frame whose footprint does not put the site
comfortably inside the imaged strip, and keeps ranked alternates per bin in case the
chosen frame turns out not to image the site after all. It refuses to continue on
fewer than four frames or less than \SI{40}{\degree} of azimuth spread.

\subsection{Stage 2, DOWNLOAD}
Fetches the raw file from the LROC node of the Planetary Data System. Each frame is
roughly \SIrange{130}{270}{\mega\byte}. Files already on disk are reused, so a re-run
costs no bandwidth.

\subsection{Stage 3, LRONAC2ISIS}
Unpacks the archive file into an ISIS cube. This is a format translation and nothing
more. It reads the archive label, decompresses the detector data, and writes a
\texttt{.cub} with an ISIS label. The pixel values are still raw counts and there is
still no geometry.
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
lronac2isis from=M1496181900LE.IMG to=M1496181900LE.cub
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}

\subsection{Stage 4, SPICEINIT}
Attaches the geometry. SPICE is NASA's system for describing where everything was and
how it was oriented at a given instant, distributed as binary files called
\emph{kernels}. This step works out, for every image line, where the spacecraft was,
which way the camera pointed, where the surface sat underneath it, and where the Sun
was. It writes all of that into the cube's label. Nothing downstream can work without
it.

The full kernel set runs well past \SI{100}{\giga\byte}, so \texttt{web=yes} queries
the USGS kernel service instead of keeping a local copy. This is the slowest step per
frame and it needs a working internet connection.
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
spiceinit from=M1496181900LE.cub web=yes
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}

\subsection{Stage 5, CAMPT}
The first moment we can ask the camera itself a question. \texttt{campt} in ground
mode takes a latitude and longitude and reports which detector sample and line it
falls on. If the site is not on this frame's detector we learn it here, before
spending the expensive steps on a frame that cannot help.

The answer needs bounding. A camera channel is 5064 samples wide, and \texttt{campt}
will report sample 6320 without complaining, because it extrapolates past the edge of
the focal plane. So the test is not whether the sample is positive. It is whether the
sample sits inside the real detector with the correlation window's worth of room to
spare.
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
campt from=M1496181900LE.cub type=ground latitude=-84.7906 longitude=29.1957
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}

\subsection{Stage 6, LRONACCAL}
Radiometric calibration. It subtracts the dark level, divides out the flat field so
every detector element responds the same way, removes the readout echo, and converts
counts into a physical reflectance quantity. After this a brightness value means the
same thing in a 2013 frame as in a 2025 one, which is what makes frames from
different years comparable at all.
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
lronaccal from=M1496181900LE.cub to=M1496181900LE.cal.cub
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}

\subsection{Stage 7, CAM2MAP}
The step that makes pixels comparable in space. It takes the camera geometry from
stage 4 and resamples the frame onto a fixed map grid, so a given pixel in the output
always corresponds to the same patch of ground no matter which orbit the frame came
from. HATI's grid is south polar stereographic at \SI{0.9}{\meter} per pixel, defined
in a small text file the script writes itself.

Polar stereographic is used because ordinary latitude and longitude are unusable this
close to the pole, where the meridians converge and a few hundred metres of ground
can span tens of degrees of longitude. The output is a level 2 cube, and it is what
everything downstream reads.
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
cam2map from=...cal.cub map=sweep_polar.map to=...lev2.cub pixres=map defaultrange=map
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}

\subsection{Stage 8, COREGISTER}
Measures how far each projected frame sits from truth. It cuts an 800 by 800 pixel
window centred on the touchdown out of both the new frame and a trusted reference
image, then cross-correlates the two to find the offset between them to a tenth of a
pixel. Then it applies that offset and correlates again to see what is left over. It
writes \texttt{coreg\_report.csv}.

\subsection{Stage 9, GATE}
The verdict. It takes the median leftover error across all measured frames and passes
at or below one pixel. This is the single number that decides whether the shadow
measurement is allowed to run at all.

\subsection{Stage 10, MANIFEST}
Writes \texttt{manifest.json}, listing every surviving frame with its Sun angle, its
file path, and its measured shift. That file is the handover to the shadow detector.

\section{Reading the console}

Every progress line has the same shape, which makes the whole log skimmable once you
know it.

\begin{center}
\ttfamily \#\#STAGE \quad NAME \quad STATE \quad detail
\end{center}

The state is one of four words. \texttt{run} means it started. \texttt{ok} means it
finished. \texttt{fail} means it did not, and the detail says why. \texttt{skip}
means it was deliberately not attempted, which during a dry run is every stage.

\subsection{The opening lines}

\begin{console}[Selection]
\begin{term}
sweep CSV: solar_sweep_-84.791_+29.196_EDRNAC4.csv
  (\cy{89 usable frames}; 541 miss the site, 66 clip it by under 600 m)
##STAGE SELECT run
pid                    az(proxy)   elev     margin  alts
nac.m1496069741le          105.7   4.00     3149 m     3
nac.m1496181900le          121.5   4.77     2371 m     3

nac.m1433078591re          217.9   3.57     1665 m     3

\end{term}
\end{console}

The archive offered 696 frames whose bounding box touches the site. Only 89 of those
actually image it with room to spare. The five picked cover \SI{112}{\degree} of Sun
azimuth, which clears the \SI{40}{\degree} minimum comfortably. \textbf{margin} is how
far inside the imaged strip the site sits, in metres. \textbf{alts} is how many backup
frames that bin is holding in reserve.

\subsection{A frame going through cleanly}

\begin{console}[Per-frame chain]
\begin{term}
##STAGE DOWNLOAD run M1433078591RE.IMG
   ... 67 MB
##STAGE DOWNLOAD \gr{ok} M1433078591RE.IMG (132 MB)
##STAGE LRONAC2ISIS \gr{ok} M1433078591RE
##STAGE SPICEINIT \gr{ok} M1433078591RE
##STAGE CAMPT \gr{ok} M1433078591RE: site at sample 1548 of 5064, line 33725
##STAGE LRONACCAL \gr{ok} M1433078591RE
##STAGE CAM2MAP \gr{ok} M1433078591RE
\end{term}
\end{console}

The \texttt{CAMPT} line is the one to actually read. Sample 1548 of 5064 means the
site lands well inside the detector's width, which is what we want. Line 33725 is how
far down the strip it sits, and these strips are tens of thousands of lines long, so
a large number there is normal. This was the one frame of the 4 September run that
went all the way through.

\subsection{A frame rejected before it costs anything}

\begin{console}[Rejection and fallback]
\begin{term}
##STAGE SPICEINIT \gr{ok} M1496434319LE
##STAGE CAMPT \rd{fail} M1496434319LE: site at sample 6053 of 5064
              -- 989 past the edge of this channel
   -> M1496434319LE does not put the site on its detector;
      trying M1496462370LE
##STAGE DOWNLOAD run M1496462370LE.IMG
\end{term}
\end{console}

This is the gate working as intended. The footprint said this frame covered the site
by \SI{1327}{\meter}, but the camera model says the site falls 989 samples beyond the
edge of the detector. The footprint describes the whole observation and cannot see the
boundary between the camera's two optics. The run drops the frame and reaches for that
bin's next alternate instead of losing the bin.

\subsection{A co-registration that measures}

\begin{limitbox}[Illustrative, not a captured run]
The gate has not been passed yet at the time of writing. The block below shows what a
pass looks like. The shape of the output and the meaning of every field are exact, but
the numbers in it are not measured results.
\end{limitbox}

\begin{console}[Coregister and gate]
\begin{term}
##STAGE COREGISTER run
##STAGE COREGISTER \gr{ok} nac.m1433078591re
        shift=(-24.10,-58.30) px  residual=0.41 px  ncc=+0.612
co-registration budget -> data/sweep/coreg_report.csv

  frames measured        : 5 of 5
  median raw shift       : 63.10 px   (57 m at the 0.9 m/px map scale)
     -- this is the SPICE pointing error, which co-registration
        exists to remove. It is not the gate.
  median residual        : \cy{0.41 px}   <-- THE GATE, passes at <= 1.00
  median aligned ncc     : +0.612
##STAGE GATE \gr{ok} median residual 0.41 px over 5 frames
\end{term}
\end{console}

Three numbers, and they mean different things. \textbf{shift} is where the frame was
found relative to the reference, and a large value here is expected and fine.
\textbf{residual} is what remains after correcting it, and that is the gate.
\textbf{ncc} is how similar the two aligned images are, on a scale from $-1$ to $+1$.
A middling ncc with a small residual is the normal result for a solar sweep, because
the two frames were lit from different directions and genuinely contain different
shadows.

\subsection{A co-registration that fails, and says why}

\begin{console}[Diagnosed failure]
\begin{term}
##STAGE COREGISTER \rd{fail} nac.m1496630688le: only 0% real data in the
        window; this frame's strip may not cover the site
   the whole cube is empty (0.0% valid) -- cam2map wrote no image data,
   so this is a projection/extent problem, not selection
\end{term}
\end{console}

The second line is the important one. An empty window has three possible causes that
need opposite fixes, so the pipeline works out which one it is rather than leaving you
to guess. It reports the cube's fill percentage, the distance from the touchdown to
the nearest real pixel, and one of three verdicts: the projection produced nothing,
the frame genuinely misses the site, or the window is being read in the wrong place.

\subsection{Every failure line in one place}

\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{5.0cm} p{5.5cm}@{}}
\toprule

& \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}What to do \\

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- gate
\section{The gate, and why it is the residual}

Co-registration produces two error numbers and they are easy to confuse. Getting this
backwards was a real mistake in this project, so it is worth stating plainly.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{6.4cm} l c@{}}
\toprule
\sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}Number & \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}What it is
& \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}Typical size & \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}Gated \\
\midrule
\texttt{shift} & Where the frame was found relative to the reference. The residual
error in the spacecraft's own knowledge of its position and pointing
& tens of \si{\px} & no \\[4pt]
\texttt{residual} & What is left after applying the shift. Our own alignment error
& under \SI{1}{\px} & \textbf{yes} \\[4pt]
\texttt{ncc} & How similar the two aligned images look, from $-1$ to $+1$
& 0.3 to 0.8 & no \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The shift is the thing co-registration exists to remove. SPICE geolocation for this
camera is good to some tens of metres before any bundle adjustment, so a shift of 60
pixels at \SI{0.9}{\meter} per pixel is an ordinary, healthy result. Gating on it
would throw away perfectly correctable frames.

What has to be small is what survives the correction. If two frames are still
misaligned by more than a pixel after correction, then a shadow appearing to move by a
pixel between them could just as easily be the frame that moved.

\begin{keepbox}[The rule]
Median residual at or below \SI{1.00}{\px} passes. Above it, stop and report it. Do
not run the shadow measurement on a stack that failed the gate, and do not adjust the
threshold to make a run pass.
\end{keepbox}

The ncc is diagnostic rather than a pass condition, and it needs reading with care. A
low ncc alongside a small residual is not a problem. It is the expected signature of a
solar sweep, where two frames of the same ground were lit from \SI{100}{\degree} apart
and therefore genuinely contain different shadows. A low ncc alongside a large
residual is a different matter, and means the correlation did not find the right peak.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- code
\section{What the code does}

Two scripts, about 900 lines together. The first asks the archive what exists. The
second turns a subset of it into aligned images.

\subsection{solar\_sweep\_query.py}

Asks the Orbital Data Explorer, NASA's query service for planetary archives, for every
frame whose footprint touches the site, then tabulates the illumination geometry of
each. The distribution of Sun azimuths across the results is the solar sweep that is
actually available for that place, which is worth knowing before committing to any
downloads.

The function that matters is \texttt{site\_margin\_m}, which answers how far inside a
frame's imaged strip the site sits. It projects the footprint polygon's corners into
metres, then measures the distance from the site to the nearest polygon edge, positive
inside and negative outside. Projecting first is the entire point. Testing the polygon
in raw degrees looks reasonable and is wrong near the pole, where a footprint edge
spanning tens of degrees of longitude is a straight line to the test and a
kilometres-wide arc on the ground. The script writes one row per frame including a
\texttt{margin\_m} column, and keeps only frames at least \SI{600}{\meter} clear by
default.

\subsection{ingest\_sweep.py}

The pipeline itself, in reading order.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{10.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}Function & \sffamily\bfseries\color{hatinavy}What it does \\
\midrule
\texttt{load\_csv} & Reads the sweep catalogue and drops frames that miss the site or
only clip it. Keeps a separate unfiltered index of everything, because a frame
rejected on its own footprint can still be the sibling channel that rescues its
partner. \\[3pt]
\texttt{select\_frames} & Bins by Sun azimuth and picks the best frame per bin, ranking
by nearness to the target elevation with a tie-break toward frames further inside the
strip. Attaches ranked alternates. Enforces the four frame and \SI{40}{\degree}
minimums. \\[3pt]
\texttt{download} & Streams the raw file from the archive, skipping anything already on
disk above \SI{10}{\mega\byte}. \\[3pt]
\texttt{isis} & One-line wrapper that runs an ISIS program as a subprocess and returns
success plus the last 400 characters of output, which is what the failure detail in the
log comes from. \\[3pt]
\texttt{cube\_dims} & Reads the real sample and line counts out of the ISIS label, so
the detector bound is measured rather than assumed. \\[3pt]
\texttt{campt\_covers} & The authoritative coverage gate. Runs \texttt{campt} in ground
mode, parses the sample and line, and returns one of four verdicts: inside, off the
sample edge, off the readout, or unknown. Unknown defers to the footprint rather than
discarding a possibly good frame. \\[3pt]
\texttt{sibling\_channel} & Swaps the camera's left optic for its right and back. The
two optics image adjacent ground swaths, so a site just past the edge of one is often
well inside the other. \\[3pt]
\texttt{lev2\_has\_site} & Checks whether an already-projected cube actually holds
pixels at the touchdown. Run on every cached product, so a known-empty file rebuilds
itself instead of being trusted forever. \\[3pt]
\texttt{process\_frame} & Drives the ISIS chain for one frame, with the \texttt{campt}
gate placed between the two cheap steps and the two expensive ones. Deletes the
intermediates on success. \\[3pt]
\texttt{where\_is\_the\_data} & Runs only when a window comes back empty. Reads the cube
decimated, finds every valid pixel, and reports which of the three causes it is. \\[3pt]
\texttt{coregister} & Cuts matching windows, masks missing pixels, mean-subtracts both,
cross-correlates for the shift, applies it, correlates again for the residual, and
computes the correlation of the aligned pair. \\[3pt]
\texttt{main} & Parses options, runs selection, and either writes a dry-run plan or
executes. Walks each bin's queue until one frame survives. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Two details inside coregister worth knowing}

ISIS marks missing pixels with negative \texttt{FLT\_MAX}, which is about
$-3.4\times10^{38}$. That value is a perfectly finite number as far as a floating point
check is concerned, so it passes an \texttt{isfinite} test and then overflows the
variance to infinity, and the correlation returns an exact zero shift with a
not-a-number error. That looks exactly like a perfect alignment. It is now masked
explicitly, and any window with less than half real data is refused outright.

The correlation runs unnormalised rather than with the library's default phase
normalisation. Phase normalisation whitens the frequency spectrum, which amplifies the
highest-frequency content. On imagery that has been resampled, and \texttt{cam2map}
resamples by interpolating, that content is numerical noise rather than signal, and the
correlation peak disappears. A deliberately planted three pixel shift comes back as
exactly zero. Unnormalised correlation recovers planted shifts to a tenth of a pixel on
smooth, noisy, sharply textured and contrast-flipped test images alike.

\subsection{Tests}

Three test files, all offline, all runnable in seconds.
\texttt{test\_footprint.py} plants strips of known geometry and pins the containment
arithmetic against the two real archive footprints that first exposed the bug.
\texttt{test\_coreg.py} plants known shifts and demands them back to a fifth of a pixel,
including the case the library default silently returns zero for.
\texttt{test\_channels.py} covers the terrain channel stack, which is a different part
of HATI.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- failures
\section{Four ways this has already failed}

Worth knowing, because most of the guards in the code exist to catch one of these, and
their messages look cryptic without the history.

\subsection{Correlating against nothing, and calling it perfect}
The first real run reported a shift of exactly $(0.00, 0.00)$ with a not-a-number error
on every frame. That reads as flawless alignment. It was the signature of correlating a
window that contained no image data, because the missing-pixel sentinel had not been
masked. Anything that cannot carry a real measurement is now refused rather than
reported as a pass.

\subsection{Trusting a bounding box}
The archive's spatial query filters on the footprint's bounding box. These strips are
long, narrow and diagonal, so near the pole the bounding box is enormously larger than
the strip. Three of the first four frames imaged nothing at the site.

\subsection{Testing the polygon in the wrong coordinate space}
The fix for that was to test the footprint polygon instead of its box, which was right
in principle and wrong in execution: the test ray-cast in raw degrees. At
\SI{84.8}{\degree} south that space is stretched about tenfold in longitude, the polygon
balloons, and nearly everything passes. All five frames of the next run passed the test
and all five came back empty. Measured properly they missed the site by
\SIrange{0.4}{3.0}{\kilo\meter}. The test now projects the vertices first and returns a
distance in metres.

\subsection{Asking the camera the right question without bounding the answer}
Then came the \texttt{campt} gate, which checked only that the sample number was
positive. A camera channel is 5064 samples wide and \texttt{campt} extrapolates past the
focal plane without erroring. The run put the site at samples 5069, 6053 and 6320 on
three frames whose footprints read as \SIrange{1.3}{2.3}{\kilo\meter} inside, and all
three projected to empty. The sample is now tested against the cube's real width.

\begin{limitbox}[The pattern]
Every one of these produced output that looked like success. That is why the pipeline
now prefers a loud refusal over a plausible number, and why the gate is printed as a
verdict rather than left in a spreadsheet for someone to interpret.
\end{limitbox}

\section{Running it}

Selection and the dry run work anywhere Python does. Everything from stage 3 onward
needs ISIS, so on Windows that means inside WSL2.

\subsection{Find out what imagery exists}
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
python scripts/solar_sweep_query.py --lat -84.7906 --lon 29.1957 --halfwidth-km 3
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}
Writes the sweep catalogue and prints how many frames actually image the site. Costs one
web request and no disk.

\subsection{See the plan without downloading anything}
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
python scripts/ingest_sweep.py --n 10
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}
The dry run is the default. It prints the frames it would fetch and writes
\texttt{data/\allowbreak sweep/\allowbreak plan.json}.

\subsection{Actually run it}
\begin{cmdbox}\begin{cmdv}
python scripts/ingest_sweep.py --n 10 --execute
\end{cmdv}
\end{cmdbox}
Budget roughly \SI{0.35}{\giga\byte} and several minutes per frame, most of it in
\texttt{spiceinit} and \texttt{cam2map}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- glossary
\section{Glossary}

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=3pt]
\item[EDR] Experiment Data Record. The rawest archived form of a frame, close to a
detector dump.
\item[DN] Digital Number. A raw detector count, before calibration. Not a brightness.
\item[cube] ISIS's file format. Image data plus a long text label holding everything
ISIS knows about the frame.
\item[level 1 and level 2] Calibrated but still in camera geometry, versus projected
onto a map grid.
\item[SPICE] NASA's system for spacecraft and planetary geometry: positions,
orientations and time.
\item[kernel] One SPICE data file. The complete set for this spacecraft runs past
\SI{100}{\giga\byte}, so we query a web service instead.
\item[PDS] Planetary Data System. NASA's archive, where the raw files are downloaded
from.
\item[ODE] Orbital Data Explorer. The search interface over the archive that the sweep
query talks to.
\item[NAC] Narrow Angle Camera on Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Two optics, each 5064
pixels wide, imaging adjacent swaths.
\item[line-scan camera] A camera with no shutter that builds an image one row at a time
as the spacecraft moves.
\item[footprint] The archive's polygon describing roughly what ground an observation
covers. Coarse, and it describes the observation rather than one optic.
\item[ortho] An image resampled so every pixel sits at its true map position, with
terrain distortion removed.
\item[polar stereographic] A map projection that behaves near a pole, where latitude and
longitude do not.
\item[phase correlation] A method for finding the offset between two images by comparing
them in the frequency domain.
\item[nodata] A reserved pixel value meaning nothing was imaged here.
\item[PVL] Parameter Value Language. The plain-text label format ISIS uses, including
the map definition file.
\item[solar sweep] A set of images of one place taken at many different Sun angles. The
core of the method.
\end{description}
